*[AIMS]: AI Management System. The organisation-wide framework — policy, roles, risk process, controls, monitoring and continual improvement — that ISO/IEC 42001 certifies. It governs how a company runs AI responsibly, not a single model, mirroring the structure of ISO 27001 (security) or ISO 9001 (quality). Certification gives a buyer or regulator independent evidence the system exists and operates.
*[Statement of Applicability]: The documented list of which AI controls an organisation applies and which it excludes, each with a justification. It is the backbone of an ISO/IEC 42001 audit because it ties every control decision back to the organisation's risk assessment, so an auditor can see what is in scope and why.
*[SoA]: Statement of Applicability. The documented list of which controls an organisation applies and excludes under ISO/IEC 42001, each justified against its risk assessment — the central artefact an auditor inspects.
*[GPAI]: General-Purpose AI model. A broadly capable model (e.g. a large language model) that can be adapted to many tasks. Under the EU AI Act its providers owe duties such as technical documentation, a copyright policy and a training-data summary; models judged to pose "systemic risk" carry extra obligations including capability evaluation and incident reporting.
*[FRIA]: Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment. An assessment that certain deployers of high-risk AI must perform under EU AI Act Article 27, analysing how the system could affect people's fundamental rights and what measures mitigate that. It is a key piece of evidence in an audit of a high-risk deployment.
*[HITL]: Human-in-the-Loop. Keeping a human in (or on) the loop of an AI decision so a person can review, override or stop it. It only counts as a real control if that human has the time, information and authority to actually change the outcome — otherwise it becomes "oversight theatre".
*[Human-in-the-Loop]: Keeping a human in (or on) the loop of an AI decision so a person can review, override or stop it. It only counts as a real control if that human can actually change the outcome in time, rather than rubber-stamping it.
*[AI-SBOM]: AI bill of materials. A documented inventory of the models, datasets, libraries and external services an AI system is built from. It gives supply-chain transparency — letting you trace, for example, which third-party model or data source a risk or licence obligation comes from.
*[Model card]: A short, structured document describing a model's intended purpose, training data, evaluation results, limitations and risks. It is a standard transparency artefact that lets reviewers judge whether a model is fit for a given use.
*[Model cards]: Short, structured documents describing a model's intended purpose, training data, evaluation results, limitations and risks — a standard transparency artefact for judging fitness for use.
*[TLPT]: Threat-Led Penetration Testing. Advanced, intelligence-driven resilience testing that simulates real attacker tactics against live systems. DORA requires it of significant financial entities, going well beyond routine vulnerability scanning.
*[Three Lines of Defence]: A governance model that separates who owns risk from who checks it: the business owns and manages risk (1st line), risk and compliance functions set policy and oversee it (2nd line), and internal audit independently assures the whole thing (3rd line). It is the standard way to show oversight is not marking its own homework.
*[ISO 31000]: The general (non-AI) international standard for risk management. ISO/IEC 23894 deliberately reuses its process — establish context, assess, treat, monitor, communicate — and adapts it to AI, so AI risk plugs into an organisation's existing enterprise-risk approach.
*[Annex III]: The EU AI Act's list of use cases that make an AI system "high-risk" (e.g. recruitment, credit scoring, biometrics, critical infrastructure). Falling under Annex III triggers the heaviest obligations — risk management, data governance, human oversight, documentation and conformity assessment.
*[Conformity assessment]: The procedure that checks a high-risk AI system meets the EU AI Act before it is placed on the market — either via the provider's internal control or, for some cases, an external "notified body". Passing it is what allows CE marking and EU-database registration.
*[Cyber uplift]: How much an AI model lowers the cost, time or skill needed to carry out a cyber-attack. It is the central metric in deciding whether a model's offensive-cyber capability has crossed a dangerous threshold.
*[Intolerable risk]: A level of risk that should be prevented outright rather than merely managed or mitigated — a "red line". The idea (central to the Berkeley CLTC work) is to define these lines concretely, in advance, instead of relying on vague assurances of safety.
*[Risk threshold]: A defined trigger point that, once crossed, demands a specific, pre-agreed response — for example pausing deployment or adding controls. Thresholds turn open-ended risk into actionable decisions.
*[Residual risk]: The risk that remains after controls and treatments have been applied. Someone accountable must formally accept it, which is why documenting residual risk (and who signed it off) matters in an audit.
*[Risk source]: The origin of a risk — for AI, things like training-data quality, model opacity, drift, autonomy or adversarial attack. ISO/IEC 23894 catalogues AI-specific risk sources so teams don't only consider the obvious ones.
*[Agentic AI]: AI that plans, calls tools and APIs, holds memory and takes autonomous, multi-step actions toward a goal — not just single text replies. Its autonomy creates new failure modes (goal drift, tool misuse, privilege escalation, cascading multi-agent effects) that ordinary model controls don't cover.
*[Oversight theatre]: Human oversight that exists on paper but cannot actually stop or change what the AI does — because the human lacks the time, information, authority or tooling to intervene. It is a common audit finding in both HITL and agentic deployments.
*[Trustworthy AI]: NIST's set of characteristics that AI should exhibit: valid and reliable, safe, secure and resilient, accountable and transparent, explainable and interpretable, privacy-enhanced, and fair with harmful bias managed. The AI RMF is organised around achieving these.
*[SC 42]: ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 42, the international subcommittee that develops the AI standards — including ISO/IEC 42001 (management system), 23894 (risk guidance), 42005 (impact assessment) and 42006 (certification bodies).
*[ICT third-party risk]: The risk a financial entity carries from its external IT, cloud and AI providers. It is a central pillar of DORA, which requires firms to manage these dependencies contractually and to plan for a provider's outage or breach.
*[Critical ICT third-party provider]: A major ICT, cloud or AI provider whose failure could threaten the stability of the financial system. DORA places such providers under direct EU oversight, recognising that concentration on a few vendors is a systemic risk.
*[AICM]: AI Controls Matrix. The Cloud Security Alliance's catalogue of AI security and governance controls, used to map agentic and other AI risks to concrete, auditable controls and to crosswalk them to other frameworks.
*[ICT risk management]: DORA's first pillar. A documented, board-owned framework to identify, protect against, detect, respond to and recover from ICT (information-and-communication-technology) risks. Financial entities must map their ICT assets and dependencies and keep the framework continually up to date.
*[ICT incident management & reporting]: DORA's pillar requiring firms to detect, log, classify and manage ICT-related incidents — and to report the *major* ones to their regulator within set deadlines using harmonised templates, so supervisors get a consistent, timely picture.
*[Digital operational resilience testing]: DORA's pillar requiring a regular programme of resilience testing — from basic vulnerability assessments and scans up to advanced threat-led penetration testing (TLPT) for the most significant entities — to prove systems withstand real-world stress and attack.
*[Information sharing]: DORA's voluntary pillar encouraging financial entities to exchange cyber-threat intelligence and indicators of compromise within trusted communities, improving the whole sector's collective resilience.
*[GOVERN]: The cross-cutting function of the NIST AI RMF — establishing the culture, policies, roles and accountability for AI risk that the other three functions depend on.
*[MAP]: The NIST AI RMF function that establishes context and frames an AI system's risks — its intended purpose, data, stakeholders and foreseeable misuse.
*[MEASURE]: The NIST AI RMF function that analyses, benchmarks and monitors AI risks and trustworthiness using quantitative and qualitative methods.
*[MANAGE]: The NIST AI RMF function that prioritises, treats, responds to and recovers from the risks surfaced by MAP and MEASURE, on an ongoing basis.
*[GenAI Profile]: NIST AI 600-1 — the companion profile to the AI RMF that enumerates twelve generative-AI-specific risks (e.g. confabulation, CBRN information, data privacy) and suggested actions.
*[AI RMF Playbook]: NIST's living companion to the AI RMF Core, giving concrete suggested actions for each subcategory of GOVERN / MAP / MEASURE / MANAGE.
*[AG-GV]: The "Govern" function of the CSA Agentic Profile — bounding an AI agent's objectives, authority and accountability.
*[AG-MP]: The "Map" function of the CSA Agentic Profile — mapping an agent's context, tools and action (blast-radius) surface.
*[AG-MS]: The "Measure" function of the CSA Agentic Profile — evaluating an agent's behaviour, reliability and the effectiveness of human oversight.
*[AG-MG]: The "Manage" function of the CSA Agentic Profile — response, containment, kill-switch and recovery for autonomous behaviour.
*[AI Growth Lab]: A UK DSIT proposal (consultation 2025–26) for cross-economy regulatory "sandboxes" that let AI be tested under targeted, time-limited relaxations of the rules.
*[Pro-innovation approach]: The UK's principles-based, sector-led model of AI regulation — existing regulators apply five shared principles rather than one central AI law.
*[Annex SL]: The common high-level structure (clauses 4–10) shared by modern ISO management-system standards such as ISO 27001, 9001 and 42001, so they integrate cleanly.
*[Capability evaluations]: Tests of what an AI model can actually do (e.g. offensive-cyber or biosecurity uplift), used to tie risk thresholds to observed capability.
