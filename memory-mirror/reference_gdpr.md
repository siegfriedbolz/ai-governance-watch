# GDPR — General Data Protection Regulation (Reg. (EU) 2016/679) for AI systems

!!! warning "Scope of this page"
    This is not a general GDPR handbook. It covers the parts of the GDPR an
    **AI risk auditor** meets in every engagement — and how they interlock
    with the [EU AI Act](reference_eu_ai_act.md). Cookie rules, international
    transfers and the one-stop-shop mechanism are out of scope.

## Summary

The General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) is the
**binding EU law on the processing of personal data**, applicable since
25 May 2018. It is technology-neutral: it does not mention artificial
intelligence, and it applies to every AI system that is trained on, prompted
with, retrieves or outputs personal data. The AI Act does not replace it —
its Art. 2(7) states that Union data-protection law applies to personal data
processed in connection with the AI Act and that the AI Act does not affect
the GDPR. For an auditor this means **two regimes on one system**: the AI Act
asks whether the *system* is safe and trustworthy; the GDPR asks whether each
*processing of personal data* around it is lawful, limited, transparent and
secure. The GDPR is also the regime that already bites in full — including
its fines of up to EUR 20 million or 4 % of worldwide annual turnover — while
the AI Act's high-risk obligations only start in December 2027 and August
2028. Three provisions carry most of the weight in AI work: **Art. 9**
(special categories, above all health data), **Art. 22** (decisions based
solely on automated processing) and **Art. 35** (the data protection impact
assessment).

## In plain language

!!! note "Our explanation, not the official text"
    Plain-language summary in our own words. EU legal text is reusable with
    attribution (EUR-Lex, Decision 2011/833/EU). Not legal advice.

If an AI system touches information about identifiable people — customers,
patients, students, employees — the GDPR applies, whatever the AI Act says
about the system's risk class. A harmless-looking chatbot that the AI Act
treats as a mere transparency case is a full GDPR case the moment a person
types their symptoms into it. You need a legal reason for every use of the
data, you must tell people what happens with it, you must keep it accurate
and secure, and you must be able to delete or correct it — which is hard
once data sits inside a model, a vector index or a prompt log. Health data,
and a few other sensitive kinds, are forbidden to process unless one of a
short list of exceptions fits. Nobody may be subjected to a purely automated
decision with serious effects unless narrow conditions are met. And before a
risky processing starts, its risks must be assessed in writing.

## Key terms

- **Personal data** — any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (Art. 4(1)); prompts, chat logs, embeddings and model outputs qualify when they relate to a person.
- **Controller / processor** — the controller decides purposes and means; the processor acts on its behalf under a contract (Arts. 4(7), 4(8), 28). The AI Act's *provider* and *deployer* are different roles: one company can be deployer and controller, and its model vendor a processor — or a controller of its own.
- **Special categories** — data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership; genetic data, biometric data for unique identification, **data concerning health**, sex life or sexual orientation (Art. 9(1)).
- **Data concerning health** — personal data related to the physical or mental health of a person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about their health status (Art. 4(15)).
- **Profiling** — automated processing to evaluate personal aspects of a person, such as performance at work, health, preferences, reliability or behaviour (Art. 4(4)).
- **DPIA (Datenschutz-Folgenabschätzung, DSFA)** — the written assessment required before processing that is likely to result in a high risk to people's rights and freedoms (Art. 35).
- **Supervisory authority** — the independent data-protection authority; in Germany the federal commissioner (BfDI) and the authorities of the Länder, which coordinate in the *Datenschutzkonferenz* (DSK).

## In depth: how the GDPR and the AI Act fit together

> **Reading guide:** the boxed **“Source text”** is the regulation's own
> wording (verbatim; EU law, reusable under EUR-Lex Decision 2011/833/EU with
> source acknowledgement). Text marked **“In our words”** is our explanation.

!!! quote "Source text — EU AI Act, Art. 2(7), as replaced by Reg. (EU) 2026/1744 (© European Union, EUR-Lex)"
    Union law on the protection of personal data, privacy and the
    confidentiality of communications applies to personal data processed in
    connection with the rights and obligations laid down in this Regulation.
    Without prejudice to Articles 4a and 59 of this Regulation, this
    Regulation shall not affect Regulation (EU) 2016/679 or (EU) 2018/1725,
    or Directive 2002/58/EC or (EU) 2016/680.

**In our words —** the two laws answer different questions and neither
satisfies the other. Five connections matter in practice:

1. **The AI Act creates no general legal basis for processing personal
   data** — it opens two narrow grounds, the two articles its Art. 2(7)
   reserves. [Art. 4a](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/4a/ "EU AI Act Article 4a, inserted by the Digital Omnibus (Reg. (EU) 2026/1744) — processing of special categories of personal data for bias detection and correction: allowed only where strictly necessary and subject to six cumulative safeguards, among them that synthetic or anonymised data would not do, pseudonymisation, strict access control, no transfer to other parties, deletion once the bias is corrected, and an entry in the GDPR record of processing explaining the necessity.")
   of the AI Act (inserted by the Digital Omnibus, which deleted the former
   Art. 10(5)) allows special-category data to be processed for **bias
   detection and correction** where strictly necessary and under six
   cumulative safeguards; para. 2 extends this to other AI systems and to
   deployers, and states that it creates no obligation to do so.
   [Art. 59](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/59/ "EU AI Act Article 59 — further processing of personal data for developing certain AI systems in the public interest in the AI regulatory sandbox: personal data lawfully collected for other purposes may be processed solely to develop, train and test such systems in the sandbox, under ten cumulative conditions, among them a substantial public interest in one of the listed areas (such as public health), no effective alternative in anonymised, synthetic or other non-personal data, a functionally separate and protected processing environment, no measures or decisions affecting the data subjects, and deletion when participation ends. Not amended by the Digital Omnibus.")
   allows personal data lawfully collected for other purposes to be
   processed **inside an AI regulatory sandbox**, solely to develop, train
   and test certain AI systems in the substantial public interest, under ten
   cumulative conditions; Recital 140 calls this the legal basis for that
   use, in accordance with GDPR Art. 6(4) and Art. 9(2)(g). Everything else
   needs a GDPR basis from Art. 6 — and, for special categories, an
   Art. 9(2) exception on top.
2. **The impact assessments are designed to be joined.** A deployer of a
   high-risk system uses the provider's Art. 13 instructions to carry out its
   DPIA (AI Act Art. 26(9)). Where the deployer must also perform a
   fundamental rights impact assessment, it may cross-refer to the relevant
   DPIA sections or include them (Art. 27(4), as replaced by the Omnibus).
   One document set, two legal anchors.
3. **Human oversight and Art. 22 are not the same test.** The AI Act's
   Art. 14 asks the provider to make oversight *possible*; GDPR Art. 22 asks
   whether the decision that reaches the person was in fact taken by a human
   with real discretion. A system can meet Art. 14 and its deployment still
   breach Art. 22.
4. **Two rights to an explanation.** GDPR Art. 15(1)(h) gives access to
   meaningful information about the logic involved in automated
   decision-making; AI Act Art. 86 gives a right to an explanation of the
   role of a high-risk Annex III system in a decision. Art. 86 sits in
   Chapter IX and has applied since 2 August 2026, but it refers to
   high-risk systems, whose classification rules apply from 2 December
   2027 — when it bites in practice is open, and we flag it rather than
   decide it.
5. **Two incident clocks.** A personal-data breach goes to the supervisory
   authority within 72 hours (Art. 33); a serious incident of a high-risk AI
   system goes to the market-surveillance authority under AI Act Art. 73 —
   and the [Cyber Resilience Act](reference_eu_cra.md) adds a third clock for
   products.

## In depth: health data and other special categories (Art. 9)

!!! quote "Source text — GDPR, Art. 9(1) (© European Union, EUR-Lex)"
    Processing of personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, political
    opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, or trade union membership,
    and the processing of genetic data, biometric data for the purpose of
    uniquely identifying a natural person, data concerning health or data
    concerning a natural person's sex life or sexual orientation shall be
    prohibited.

**In our words —** the rule is a prohibition with a closed list of exceptions
in Art. 9(2). For AI in a health or care setting three of them do the work:

- **Explicit consent** for specified purposes (Art. 9(2)(a)) — the default
  for an assistant or chatbot that patients or customers use voluntarily. The
  German supervisory authorities' orientation aid gives the example of
  AI-supported skin diagnostics in a medical practice: consent there must be
  preceded by information and explanations on how the specific AI
  application works (para. 63). Applying that expectation to other AI
  applications is our reading.
- **Medical diagnosis, health or social care or treatment** on the basis of
  Union or Member State law or a contract with a health professional
  (Art. 9(2)(h)) — only where the data are processed by or under the
  responsibility of a professional bound by **professional secrecy**
  (Art. 9(3)).
- **Public interest in public health**, including high standards of quality
  and safety of health care and of medicinal products or medical devices, on
  the basis of law with suitable safeguards (Art. 9(2)(i)).

Member States may add further conditions for genetic, biometric and health
data (Art. 9(4)) — the German ones are below. Three consequences an auditor
checks first:

1. **Free-text input is the leak.** A product-advice or support chatbot
   processes health data as soon as users describe symptoms, diagnoses or
   medication — whether or not the operator intended it. The supervisory
   authorities' ChatGPT taskforce put it plainly: a public service should
   assume that people will enter personal data, and the operator cannot shift
   its responsibility to users through its terms of use (report of 23 May
   2024, paras. 24–25; see [Guidance](#guidance-and-case-law-edpb-and-cjeu)). The
   design question is what happens to that text: stored in logs, sent to a
   model vendor, reused for training, retrievable by other users. The
   proposed AI derogation in the Digital Omnibus would not change this: the
   EDPB and the EDPS read it as covering only incidental and residual
   special-category data in the development of a model, **not** data
   collected through prompts during deployment (Joint Opinion 2/2026,
   paras. 48–49), and the Council's negotiating mandate says the same in its
   recital 33: the derogation does not cover special-category data directly
   acquired by or provided to the system in the course of deployment (see
   [Pending amendments](#pending-amendments) and
   [Guidance](#guidance-and-case-law-edpb-and-cjeu)).
2. **Automated decisions on special categories are barred twice.** Art. 22(4)
   allows a solely automated decision based on special-category data only
   with explicit consent or a substantial public interest laid down in law,
   and with safeguards in place.
3. **Scale triggers the DPIA.** Large-scale processing of special categories
   is one of the three cases in which Art. 35(3) requires a DPIA by name —
   and in Germany the supervisory authorities' list goes further (see
   [Germany](#germany-bdsg-and-the-datenschutzkonferenz-dsk)).

### Pending amendments: Commission proposal and Council mandate {#pending-amendments}

Read at source on 2026-11-12. **Neither text is law.** The Commission's
proposal is [COM(2025) 837](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52025PC0837);
the Council's position is the mandate for negotiations with Parliament agreed
by the Permanent Representatives Committee on 21 October 2026
([Council document 14756/26](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-14756-2026-INIT/en/pdf) — it reproduces the proposal and marks
the Council's additions and deletions; Art. 3 amends the GDPR). Parliament has
not adopted a position. The points that matter for AI:

| Topic | Commission proposal | Council mandate |
| ----- | ------------------- | --------------- |
| Definition of personal data (Art. 4(1)) | Adds sentences that make the test relative to the entity holding the information. | **Deletes the addition.** Instead a new **Art. 25a** on pseudonymisation: pseudonymised data are not personal data for a third party that cannot identify the person; for a processor they remain personal data; the EDPB is to issue an opinion on pseudonymisation and anonymisation. |
| Special categories in AI (Art. 9(2)(k), 9(5)) | A derogation for processing in the development and operation of an AI system or AI model; data that are found are to be removed, or protected where removal needs disproportionate effort. | Narrowed to **incidental processing of residual data, not intended by the controller**, in the development and *technical* operation of a system or model. Data that are found are **erased without undue delay**; where erasure is technically impossible or manifestly disproportionate, they are protected from further processing, from use to produce outputs and from disclosure. Recital 33: data directly acquired by or provided to the system during deployment are not covered. |
| Legitimate interest for AI | A new Art. 88c: processing for the development and operation of AI may be pursued for legitimate interests under Art. 6(1)(f), with safeguards that include an unconditional right to object. | Kept in shorter form (renumbered "88 bis"): such processing *may* be carried out for a legitimate interest; the controller still has to choose an appropriate legal basis from Art. 6; whatever the basis, safeguards apply, including the right to object under Art. 21. Recital 33a: developing or operating AI is **not in itself** a legitimate interest — it is assessed case by case. |
| Automated decisions (Art. 22) | Recasts paragraph 1 as a permission (a decision *may* be based solely on automated processing only in three cases) and adds that the contract case applies whether or not a human could take the decision. | Restores the wording as the data subject's **right not to be subject** to such a decision and deletes that addition from the article; recital 38 keeps both points of the Commission's recital: the decision need not be one only a machine could take, and the less intrusive of equally effective automated solutions is to be used. |
| Breach notification (Art. 33) | Notification only where a high risk is likely, within 96 hours instead of 72, through a single-entry point. | Same threshold and deadline, with the support of a national entry point; the EDPB publishes lists of circumstances that are, and are not, likely to mean a high risk. |
| DPIA lists (Art. 35) | EU-wide lists of processing that does or does not need a DPIA, proposed by the EDPB and adopted by the Commission. | EU-wide lists established and published by the EDPB itself; national lists stay valid until then; a common DPIA template and methodology by implementing act. |

Until an amending act is published in the Official Journal, the GDPR applies as
written: 72 hours in Art. 33, the national DPIA lists, and Art. 22 in its
current wording.

## Key Sections

- **[Art. 5 — Principles](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-5-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 5 — lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; and accountability: the controller must be able to demonstrate compliance.")** — lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; accountability. For AI the hard ones are accuracy (hallucinated statements about a person), purpose limitation (reusing operational data for training) and storage limitation (data inside a model or index).
- **[Art. 6 — Lawfulness of processing](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-6-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 6 — the six legal bases: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, legitimate interests; legitimate interests requires a three-step test of interest, necessity and balancing.")** — the six legal bases; legitimate interests (Art. 6(1)(f)) requires the three-step test of interest, necessity and balancing that the EDPB applies to AI models; the EDPB's general guidelines on this basis are final since 10 November 2026 (Guidelines 1/2024, version 2.0 — see below).
- **[Art. 9 — Special categories](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 9 — processing of special categories, among them health, genetic and biometric data, is prohibited unless one of the exceptions in paragraph 2 applies; paragraph 3 ties the health-care exception to professional secrecy; paragraph 4 lets Member States add conditions.")** — prohibition with a closed list of exceptions; see the in-depth section above.
- **[Arts. 13–15 — Information and access](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-15-gdpr/ "GDPR Articles 13 to 15 — information to be given when data are collected and the right of access; each includes the existence of automated decision-making, including profiling, and meaningful information about the logic involved, its significance and envisaged consequences.")** — what people must be told, including the existence of automated decision-making and meaningful information about the logic involved.
- **[Art. 22 — Automated individual decision-making](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-22-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 22 — the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects or similarly significantly affects the person; exceptions for contract, law or explicit consent, with the right to human intervention, to express a view and to contest; special categories only under paragraph 4.")** — no decision based solely on automated processing with legal or similarly significant effects, unless contract, law or explicit consent allows it and safeguards exist.
- **[Art. 25 — Data protection by design and by default](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-25-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 25 — the controller implements technical and organisational measures, such as pseudonymisation and data minimisation, at the time the means of processing are determined and at the time of processing itself.")** and **[Art. 32 — Security of processing](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-32-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 32 — appropriate technical and organisational measures for a level of security appropriate to the risk, including confidentiality, integrity, availability and resilience, and regular testing.")** — the engineering duties: permissions enforced in retrieval, tenant separation, prompt and log retention, protection against extraction.
- **[Art. 28 — Processor](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 28 — processing on behalf of a controller requires a contract that binds the processor to documented instructions, confidentiality, security, sub-processor rules and deletion or return of the data.")** — the contract a model or hosting vendor needs; whether the vendor uses inputs for its own purposes decides whether it is a processor at all.
- **[Art. 33 — Breach notification](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-33-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 33 — a personal-data breach is notified to the supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless it is unlikely to result in a risk; the processor notifies the controller without undue delay.")** — 72 hours to the supervisory authority.
- **[Art. 35 — Data protection impact assessment](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-35-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 35 — a DPIA is required before processing likely to result in a high risk, in particular when using new technologies; always for systematic and extensive automated evaluation with significant effects, large-scale processing of special categories, and large-scale systematic monitoring of public areas; minimum content in paragraph 7.")** and **[Art. 36 — Prior consultation](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-36-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 36 — where the DPIA shows a high risk that the controller cannot mitigate, the supervisory authority must be consulted before processing starts.")** — the written risk assessment before processing starts, and consultation of the authority where the risk stays high.
- **[Art. 83 — Fines](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-83-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 83 — administrative fines up to EUR 20 million or 4 % of total worldwide annual turnover for breaches of the principles (Arts. 5, 6, 7, 9), data-subject rights (Arts. 12 to 22) and transfer rules; up to EUR 10 million or 2 % for controller and processor obligations such as Arts. 25 to 39.")** — up to **EUR 20 million or 4 %** of worldwide annual turnover for breaches of the principles, Arts. 6 and 9 and the data-subject rights; up to **EUR 10 million or 2 %** for the controller and processor obligations in Arts. 25–39.

## Guidance and case law (EDPB and CJEU)

Not legislation, but what supervisory authorities and courts apply:

- **[EDPB Opinion 28/2024 on AI models (2024-12-17)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/2024-12/edpb_opinion_202428_ai-models_en.pdf "EDPB Opinion 28/2024 on certain data protection aspects related to the processing of personal data in the context of AI models, adopted 17 December 2024 at the request of the Irish supervisory authority under Art. 64(2): when a model can be considered anonymous, legitimate interest as a legal basis for development and deployment, and the consequences of unlawful processing in the development phase.")** — a model trained on personal data is **not automatically anonymous**: both the likelihood of extracting training data and the likelihood of obtaining it through queries must be insignificant, assessed case by case. Legitimate interest can be a basis for development and deployment if the three-step test is passed; the Opinion also addresses what unlawful training means for later use of the model.
- **[EDPB Guidelines 1/2024 on processing based on Art. 6(1)(f) (version 2.0, 2026-11-10)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-11/edpb_guidelines_202401_legitimateinterest_v2_en.pdf "EDPB Guidelines 1/2024 on processing of personal data based on Article 6(1)(f) GDPR, version 2.0, adopted on 10 November 2026 after public consultation (version 1.0 of 8 October 2024 was the consultation version): the three cumulative conditions of legitimate interest, the method for the balancing test, data-subject rights and typical contexts; para. 90 refers to Opinion 28/2024 for the development and deployment of AI models.")** — **final**. The general method for legitimate interest: a legitimate interest is pursued, the processing is necessary for it, and the interests and rights of the data subject do not take precedence — the three conditions are cumulative. For AI the guidelines refer to Opinion 28/2024 and name as interests that can be legitimate a conversational agent that assists users, the detection of fraudulent content or behaviour, and threat detection in an information system (para. 90).
- **[EDPB Guidelines 03/2026 on web scraping in the context of generative AI (version 1.0, 2026-07-07)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-07/edpb_guidelines_2020603_webscraping_v1_en_0.pdf "EDPB Guidelines 03/2026, version 1.0, adopted on 7 July 2026 for public consultation (closed 30 October 2026): the GDPR applies to scraping that includes personal data; purpose limitation, transparency, data minimisation and accuracy measures; legitimate interest with the three-step test; special categories are in principle prohibited. Limited to scraping by private entities.")** — **consultation version, not final**: the GDPR applies to scraping that includes personal data; that people made data accessible online does not mean they consented to its scraping (para. 45); lists minimisation and mitigation measures for building training sets.
- **[EDPB Guidelines 02/2026 on anonymisation (version 1.0, 2026-07-07)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-09/edpb_guidelines_202602_anonymisation_v1_en_0.pdf "EDPB Guidelines 02/2026 on Anonymisation, version 1.0, adopted on 7 July 2026 for public consultation (closed 30 October 2026): clarifies the notion of anonymous data and gives a practical framework for assessing it.")** — **consultation version, not final**: when data stop being personal data — the question behind every claim that a dataset or model is out of scope.
- **[EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 2/2026 on the Digital Omnibus (2026-02-10)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/2026-02/edpb_edps_jointopinion_202602_digitalomnibus_en.pdf "EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 2/2026 on the Proposal for a Regulation as regards the simplification of the digital legislative framework (Digital Omnibus), adopted 10 February 2026. Section 6.1: a specific provision on legitimate interest for AI (proposed Art. 88c) is not necessary because Opinion 28/2024 already confirms it under the current text; paras. 48–49: the proposed Art. 9(2)(k) should be limited in the enacting terms to incidental and residual processing and should not be understood as covering special-category data collected through prompts during deployment.")** — the supervisory authorities' view of the proposed GDPR changes for AI: a special legitimate-interest article is unnecessary, and the proposed derogation for special-category data should stay confined to **incidental and residual** data in development — not data entered through prompts. Not binding, and about a proposal; it shows how the authorities read the law as it stands.
- **[EDPB template for data protection impact assessments (version 1.0, adopted 2026-03-10)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/public-consultations/template-for-data-protection-impact-assessment_en "EDPB Template [2026] for Data Protection Impact Assessment, version 1.0, adopted on 10 March 2026 for public consultation, published 14 April 2026 with an explainer; feedback period 14 April to 9 June 2026. Parts 0 to 6: overview of the processing, systematic description, analysis (lawfulness, minimisation, measures), necessity and proportionality, risk assessment and management with inherent and residual risk, involvement of the DPO and of data subjects, conclusion and decision.")** — **consultation version**; on 2026-11-12 the EDPB register lists no final version. A fill-in structure in seven parts, from the overview of the processing to a documented decision (rejected, consultation of the authority, approved, conditionally approved). Use is voluntary; the EDPB states that after the consultation the authorities will adopt it as their only template or as a meta-template for national ones. It has no AI-specific part — an innovative use of technology is one of the reasons for a DPIA it lists — so the AI specifics still come from the DSK aids and Opinion 28/2024.
- **[EDPB template for personal data breach notification (version 1.0, adopted 2026-06-08)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/public-consultations/template-for-personal-data-breach-notification_en "EDPB Template [2026] for personal data breach notification, version 1.0, adopted on 8 June 2026 for public consultation; feedback period 10 June to 5 August 2026. Seven parts: type of notification, controller and reporting person, initial information on the breach, further information including likely consequences and risk assessment, communication to data subjects, other issues such as cross-border processing, attachments.")** — **consultation version**. Designed to be implemented by the supervisory authorities in an IT tool, with fields, tooltips and rules for what is asked when; the EDPB will decide on the timeline for its use after the consultation. For an incident involving an AI system it shows what an authority will ask: nature and circumstances, categories and numbers of people and records, measures in place, likely consequences, risk assessment, measures taken, communication to the people affected.
- **[EDPB coordinated enforcement action 2027 (EDPB news, 2026-11-11)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/news/edpb-adopts-statement-on-review-of-the-republic-of-korea-adequacy-processor-bcr_en "EDPB news of 11 November 2026: the Board selected the topic of its sixth coordinated enforcement action — automated decision-making, including AI-driven decision-making, in recruitment; focus on the right not to be subject to decisions based solely on automated processing and the related right of access in automated candidate screening. Authorities join voluntarily; launch in the course of 2027.")** — the supervisory authorities will examine **automated decision-making in recruitment, including AI-driven screening of candidates**: the right not to be subject to a solely automated decision (Art. 22) and the related right of access. Participation is voluntary for each authority; the action starts in the course of 2027. Not guidance, but a statement of where enforcement will look.
- **[EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 1/2026 on the Digital Omnibus on AI (adopted 2026-01-20)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-04/edpb_edps_jointopinion_202601_proposal_ai-omnibus_en.pdf "EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 1/2026 on the Proposal for a Regulation as regards the simplification of the implementation of harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Digital Omnibus on AI), adopted 20 January 2026: support in principle for extending the bias-detection ground (new Art. 4a) with a recommendation to limit it for non-high-risk systems and to keep strict necessity; keep the registration obligation for Art. 6(3) systems; EU-level sandboxes, AI Office supervision, AI literacy and the high-risk timeline. The executive summary states that the EDPB and the Commission are working on joint guidelines on the interplay between the GDPR and the AI Act.")** — the authorities' view of the AI Act changes that the Omnibus later enacted. Two points matter here: they supported the new bias-detection ground in principle but asked to keep **strict necessity** and to limit its use outside high-risk systems — the adopted Art. 4a(1) does say *strictly necessary* for high-risk systems — and they stated that the EDPB and the Commission **are working on joint guidelines on the interplay between the GDPR and the AI Act**. On 2026-11-12 no such guidelines are in the EDPB register — a watch item.
- **[EDPB Statement 3/2024 on the role of data protection authorities in the AI Act framework (2024-07-16)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/2024-07/edpb_statement_202403_dpasroleaiact_en.pdf "EDPB Statement 3/2024, adopted 16 July 2024: DPAs must be market surveillance authorities for the high-risk systems named in Art. 74(8) AI Act; the EDPB recommends that Member States consider them for the other Annex III systems that affect natural persons' rights, and as single points of contact; it expects a close relationship between the DPIA and the fundamental rights impact assessment (Arts. 26(9), 27(4), Annex VIII Section C point 5).")** — under AI Act Art. 74(8), data protection authorities (or authorities with equivalent independence) must supervise high-risk systems of Annex III point 1 used for law enforcement, border management, justice and democracy, and those of points 6, 7 and 8. For the rest of Annex III the EDPB recommends that Member States consider them too. For an auditor this means: where the DPA is also the AI market surveillance authority, one authority reads the DPIA and the AI Act file side by side.
- **[Report of the ChatGPT taskforce (2024-05-23)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/documents/2024-05/edpb_20240523_report_chatgpt_taskforce_en.pdf "Report of the work undertaken by the ChatGPT Taskforce, 23 May 2024: preliminary views agreed as the common denominator of the supervisory authorities investigating OpenAI's ChatGPT service; on lawfulness of training data and of prompts, fairness, transparency and accuracy. Does not prejudge the individual investigations.")** — interim views of the supervisory authorities, the common denominator of their investigations into one service, not a decision. Three points travel to any public chatbot: users must be told clearly that their input may be used for training (paras. 22, 28); the operator stays responsible for personal data users type in and cannot push that responsibility onto them through its terms (paras. 24–25); and the **accuracy principle applies to the output**, which users are likely to take as fact (paras. 29–30).
- **[EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 3/2026 on the European Biotech Act (adopted 2026-03-10)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-04/edpb_edps_jointopinion_202603_biotechact_en.pdf "EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 3/2026 on the Proposal for a European Biotech Act, adopted 10 March 2026: among other points, obligations for sponsors that intend to use AI models or systems in clinical trials, which the authorities want to apply in addition to the AI Act, and EMA guidance on AI in the lifecycle of medicinal products, for which the EMA may consult the EDPB.")** — for health: the proposal would oblige sponsors that intend to use AI models or systems in a clinical trial to meet specific duties; the authorities recommend making clear that these apply **in addition to** the AI Act. A proposal, not law.
- **[Guidelines 1/2026 on processing for scientific research purposes (consultation version, adopted 2026-04-15)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-05/edpb_guidelines_202601_scientificresearch_en.pdf "EDPB Guidelines 1/2026 on processing of personal data for scientific research purposes, version for public consultation adopted 15 April 2026 (consultation closed 25 June 2026): six key-indicative factors for genuine scientific research, presumption of purpose compatibility, broad and dynamic consent, legitimate interest for research including commercial research, special categories, transparency, rights, roles, and safeguards under Art. 89(1) — anonymised or pseudonymised data first.")** — **consultation version**. Research counts as scientific research under the GDPR if it meets six factors: methodical and systematic approach, ethical standards, verifiable and transparent results, autonomy and independence of qualified researchers, the aim of adding to general knowledge (commercial aims not excluded), and the potential to contribute to knowledge (para. 11); research that meets all six is presumed to qualify (para. 12). One worked example is a start-up researching bias in generative AI models together with a university. For health research the safeguards start with a risk analysis or DPIA that looks beyond privacy, including what happens when a medical condition is found (para. 151).
- **[Guidelines 01/2025 on pseudonymisation (consultation version, adopted 2025-01-16)](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2025-01/edpb_guidelines_202501_pseudonymisation_en.pdf)** — still listed as the version for public consultation; the Council's mandate on the Digital Omnibus would add an article on pseudonymisation and ask the EDPB for an opinion on it (see [Pending amendments](#pending-amendments)).

Not re-read.

## Germany: BDSG and the Datenschutzkonferenz (DSK)

The GDPR applies directly; German law adds to it where the GDPR leaves room:

- **[§ 22 BDSG — special categories](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bdsg_2018/__22.html "Section 22 of the Federal Data Protection Act (BDSG): by way of derogation from Art. 9(1) GDPR, processing of special categories is permitted, among other cases, for preventive health care, medical diagnosis, care or treatment in the health or social sector or under a contract with a health professional, where the data are processed by medical staff or other persons under a corresponding duty of secrecy, or under their responsibility; and for public-health reasons such as quality and safety of health care, medicinal products and medical devices.")** — the German implementation of Art. 9(2)(h) and (i): health-care processing by, or under the responsibility of, staff bound by a duty of secrecy.
- **[§ 38 BDSG — data protection officer](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bdsg_2018/__38.html "Section 38 BDSG: controllers and processors designate a data protection officer where, as a rule, at least 20 persons are constantly engaged in automated processing of personal data — and, regardless of headcount, where they carry out processing that is subject to a DPIA under Art. 35 GDPR.")** — a data protection officer is mandatory **regardless of headcount** once a processing is subject to a DPIA. For a small company that introduces an AI system on health data, this is often the first formal consequence.
- **[DSK list of processing operations that require a DPIA — non-public sector (version 1.1, 2018-10-17)](https://datenschutz-hamburg.de/fileadmin/user_upload/HmbBfDI/Datenschutz/Informationen/DSFA_Muss-Liste_fuer_den_nicht-oeffentlicher_Bereich_-_Stand_17.10.2018.pdf "Liste der Verarbeitungstätigkeiten, für die eine DSFA durchzuführen ist — the list under Art. 35(4) GDPR agreed among the members of the Datenschutzkonferenz for controllers in the non-public sector, version 1.1 of 17 October 2018, issued after the EDPB's Opinion 5/2018 of 25 September 2018 on the German draft (copy published by the Hamburg supervisory authority; the public sector has separate lists per authority). No. 11: use of artificial intelligence to process personal data in order to steer the interaction with data subjects or to evaluate personal aspects of the data subject — examples: customer support by AI, a call centre evaluating callers' mood, a system that advises customers in conversation. No. 16: telemedicine solutions processing health data collected by sensors or mobile applications. No. 17: Art. 9 data from sensors used to determine a person's performance.")** — the "Muss-Liste" under Art. 35(4) for companies and other non-public controllers. **No. 11** makes a DPIA mandatory for the *use of AI to process personal data to steer the interaction with data subjects or to evaluate their personal aspects*; its examples are AI customer support and a system that advises customers in conversation. For a company's customer-facing chatbot that processes personal data in Germany, the DPIA is therefore not a judgement call; acting without one can be fined under Art. 83(4). Nos. 16 and 17 add telemedicine and sensor-based health data.
- **[DSK orientation aid *Künstliche Intelligenz und Datenschutz* (version 1.0, 2024-05-06)](https://www.datenschutzkonferenz-online.de/media/oh/20240506_DSK_Orientierungshilfe_KI_und_Datenschutz.pdf "Orientierungshilfe der Konferenz der unabhängigen Datenschutzaufsichtsbehörden des Bundes und der Länder vom 6. Mai 2024, Version 1.0: criteria for selecting, implementing and using AI applications, focused on LLM-based applications; addressed to controllers that deploy AI.")** — the checklist German authorities expect a deployer to have worked through. Three positions worth knowing: a DPIA will be required **in many cases** when AI applications are used (para. 39); a human must have real discretion — a merely formal human step does not satisfy Art. 22 (section 1.6); and for AI-supported diagnostics in a medical practice, Art. 9(2)(h) with the treatment contract can fit where the application meets the professional standard and is approved as a medical device, otherwise explicit consent is the route (para. 63).
- **[DSK orientation aid on technical and organisational measures for AI systems (version 1.0, June 2025)](https://datenschutzkonferenz-online.de/media/oh/DSK-OH_KI-Systeme.pdf "Orientierungshilfe zu empfohlenen technischen und organisatorischen Maßnahmen bei der Entwicklung und beim Betrieb von KI-Systemen, Version 1.0, Stand Juni 2025: requirements along the lifecycle phases design, development, deployment, and operation and monitoring.")** — measures by lifecycle phase (design, development, deployment, operation and monitoring); the German counterpart to an engineering control catalogue.
- **[DSK orientation aid on generative AI systems with RAG (version 1.0, October 2025)](https://www.datenschutzkonferenz-online.de/media/oh/DSK_OH_RAG.pdf "Orientierungshilfe zu datenschutzrechtlichen Besonderheiten generativer KI-Systeme mit RAG-Methode, Version 1.0, Stand Oktober 2025: how the GDPR principles — accuracy, transparency, integrity and confidentiality, purpose limitation, minimisation and storage limitation, lawfulness, data-subject rights — can be met in RAG systems; names tenant or functional separation and a rights-and-roles concept.")** — RAG can improve accuracy and traceability and allow smaller, on-premise models, but it creates its own duties: **tenant or functional separation** and a **rights-and-roles concept** for the retrieved documents. The data-protection assessment of the underlying model's training stays untouched.

## Audit-Relevant Anchors

- **[Art. 9 (special categories)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-9-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 9 — the prohibition and its exceptions; for health settings explicit consent (2)(a), health care under professional secrecy (2)(h) with (3), and public health (2)(i).")** — the first question wherever health, biometric or similar data can enter a system, including through free-text input.
- **[Art. 22 (automated decisions)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-22-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 22 — decisions based solely on automated processing; read with CJEU C-634/21 SCHUFA: a score counts as the decision where the recipient draws strongly on it.")** + **CJEU C-634/21** — whether the human in the loop decides or only signs; the evidence is in override rates, time per case and what the reviewer is shown.
- **[Art. 35 (DPIA)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-35-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 35 — the data protection impact assessment; minimum content: description of the processing and purposes, necessity and proportionality, risks to rights and freedoms, and measures.")** + **AI Act [Art. 26(9)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/ "EU AI Act Article 26(9) — deployers of high-risk AI systems use the information provided under Art. 13 to comply with their DPIA obligation under Art. 35 GDPR.")** / **[Art. 27(4)](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/27/ "EU AI Act Article 27(4), as replaced by the Digital Omnibus — where obligations of the fundamental rights impact assessment are already met through the DPIA, the deployer may cross-refer to the relevant DPIA sections or include them.")** — one assessment file serving both laws; an AI system on personal data without a DPIA, or without a documented reason why none is needed, is a finding today; in Germany No. 11 of the DSK list makes the DPIA mandatory for AI that steers the interaction with data subjects or evaluates their personal aspects.
- **[Art. 15(1)(h) (access to the logic)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-15-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 15(1)(h) — the right to know of automated decision-making and to receive meaningful information about the logic involved; read with CJEU C-203/22.")** + **CJEU C-203/22** — whether the operator can actually explain a specific result; a logging and traceability question before it is a legal one.
- **[Art. 28 (processor contract)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/ "GDPR Article 28 — the processing agreement; for AI vendors the decisive clauses are use of inputs for the vendor's own purposes, sub-processors, retention and deletion.")** — model, hosting and tooling vendors: is input used for training, where is it processed, who are the sub-processors, when is it deleted.
- **[Arts. 25 and 32 (design and security)](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-32-gdpr/ "GDPR Articles 25 and 32 — data protection by design and by default, and security appropriate to the risk.")** — the technical evidence: document-level permissions enforced at retrieval time, separation of tenants, retention of prompts, traces and embeddings, protection against extraction of training or retrieval data.
- **AI Act [Art. 4a](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/4a/ "EU AI Act Article 4a — special categories of personal data for bias detection and correction, under six cumulative safeguards. One of the two articles that Art. 2(7) reserves; the other is Art. 59, further processing inside a regulatory sandbox.")** — the AI Act's own ground for special-category data in bias detection and correction (the other article Art. 2(7) reserves is Art. 59, inside a regulatory sandbox); six cumulative safeguards, and the reason must be recorded in the GDPR record of processing.
- **[Pending change: Digital Omnibus, data part (COM(2025) 837)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52025PC0837 "Commission proposal of 19 November 2025 amending, among others, Regulation (EU) 2016/679: for AI a new Art. 88c on legitimate interest and a new Art. 9(2)(k) with Art. 9(5) on residual special-category data, besides changes to the definition of personal data, to information duties, to breach notification and to DPIA lists. Council negotiating mandate agreed 21 October 2026 (document 14756/26) — it keeps the current definition of personal data and narrows both AI provisions; Parliament procedure 2025/0360(COD), joint committee ITRE-LIBE, at 'awaiting committee decision' on 12 November 2026. None of it is law; the GDPR applies as written.")** — proposed amendments to the GDPR, among them a legitimate-interest article for AI (Art. 88c) and a derogation for residual special-category data in AI development (Art. 9(2)(k)); the [Council's mandate](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-14756-2026-INIT/en/pdf) of 21 October 2026 keeps the current definition of personal data and narrows both AI provisions (see [Pending amendments](#pending-amendments)). **Not law**: cite the GDPR as written and name this as a watch item, not as a basis.

## Auditor Checklist

Evidence-oriented checks for an AI system that processes personal data:

- [ ] Every personal-data flow of the system is mapped: training or fine-tuning data, retrieval corpus, prompts and uploads, outputs, logs and traces, vendor calls — with controller and processor roles named per flow.
- [ ] Each flow has a documented legal basis (Art. 6); where legitimate interest is used, the three-step test is written down.
- [ ] Special categories are identified, including those that arrive through free-text input; an Art. 9(2) exception is documented for each — for health data in Germany with § 22 BDSG where relied on.
- [ ] A DPIA exists, or a documented pre-check explains why none is required (Art. 35; in Germany checked against the DSK list, in particular No. 11; the EDPB template, a consultation version, offers a structure); for high-risk AI systems it uses the provider's Art. 13 information and is linked to the FRIA where one is due.
- [ ] No decision with legal or similarly significant effect is taken solely by the system; where a human decides, there is evidence of real discretion (Art. 22, C-634/21).
- [ ] Where the system screens, ranks or rejects job candidates: the Art. 22 safeguards and a working access route are evidenced — the EDPB's 2027 coordinated enforcement action targets automated decision-making in recruitment.
- [ ] People are told that AI is used, for what, and — where Art. 22 applies — about the logic involved (Arts. 13–15); a specific result can be explained on request (C-203/22).
- [ ] Access, rectification and erasure work in practice for prompts, logs, retrieval indices and, where applicable, the model; the limits are documented.
- [ ] Vendors are bound by an Art. 28 agreement; use of inputs for the vendor's own purposes is excluded or separately justified; processing locations and sub-processors are known.
- [ ] Retrieval enforces the permissions of the source documents; tenants or functional areas are separated; a rights-and-roles concept exists (DSK orientation aid on RAG).
- [ ] Retention periods exist for prompts, traces and embeddings; a breach process meets the 72-hour clock (Art. 33) and is aligned with AI Act Art. 73 and, for products, CRA Art. 14.
- [ ] A claim that data or a model is anonymous is supported by an assessment, not by assertion (EDPB Opinion 28/2024).
- [ ] In Germany: a data protection officer is designated where a DPIA-relevant processing exists (§ 38 BDSG).
